Coal Country and the 2016 Election

How did the importance of coal as an employer in rural Ohio and Pennsylvania affect the 2016 U.S. presidential election?

1 · The Electoral College

Swing states decided 2016

Blue states went Democratic in the Electoral College and red states went Republican. Ohio and Pennsylvania are outlined. They are the two critical swing states this story focuses on.

The Electoral College and “swing states”

Donald Trump did not win the popular vote for U.S. President in 2016. He won on electoral votes alone, which has happened only four times in the history of electing 45 U.S. Presidents.

The key to this result is swing states. From one presidential election to the next, many states reliably vote Democratic or Republican; these are their traditional strongholds. About a dozen others can go either way depending on the hot-button issues and the mood of the time. They are also called battleground states, because that is where the election is won or lost.

Four swing states flipped from Obama (Democrat) in 2012 to Trump (Republican) in 2016: Iowa (6 votes), Wisconsin (10), Ohio (18), and Pennsylvania (20). These would have been prime targets for the Trump campaign's promises.

This piece zooms in on the two with the most active coal mines, Ohio and Pennsylvania. Together they hold most of those flipped electoral votes: 38 of 54, or 70%. Wisconsin and Iowa have no significant coal mining, so they are left out.

2 · Coal employment

Coal jobs fell almost everywhere

Each coal-producing state is colored by the change in coal mining employment from 2014 to 2015. Hover over a state to see how much its number of mines and its mining workforce fell.

Promises of coal jobs

Among the major coal states, Ohio lost the largest share of its coal jobs (−21%), and Pennsylvania (−16%) and its neighbors Kentucky, West Virginia, and Indiana were not far behind. During the campaign, the Chicago Tribune covered the fate of coal mining in Illinois, which was also declining, though far less dramatically:

Coal's decline has become an issue in the presidential race. Hillary Clinton stirred fear in the mines when she told a town hall in March that the shift to clean, renewable energy means “we're going to put a lot of coal miners and coal companies out of business.”

She added that she wants to bring new economic opportunities to coal country, but the damage was done: Donald Trump, who has vowed to “bring those miners back” by scrapping regulations, is seen by some as the industry's last hope.

“I think (the election) is pivotal,” said Bob Sandidge, owner of a mining services company and founder of an advocacy group called Coal Miner's Movement. “Hillary said she's going to carry on with the clean power plan, and that's going to be the final nail in the coffin.”

3 · Coal counties

Where the mines are

This scene shows coal mining by county. Darker counties produced more coal. Hover over a county to see its total number of mines (surface and underground) and the tons it produced.

Coal country in Ohio and Pennsylvania

Given their economic situation and the candidates' campaign promises, true or not, voters in Ohio's and Pennsylvania's coal counties had strong reasons to choose Trump over Clinton.

Ohio had 25 active mines in 2015, surface and underground, producing 17 million short tons. Pennsylvania had 195 mines producing 50 million short tons. Together that is 20% of the coal produced east of the Mississippi River.

“Trump Digs Coal”

Campaigning in these coal-producing states, Trump promised to bring back the glory days of coal, even though global economic forces matter far more than his promises. His team handed out signs reading “Trump Digs Coal,” and he said that if he was elected, “We're going to put the miners back to work.”

In June 2017, Trump's EPA administrator, Scott Pruitt, claimed the coal sector had added “almost 50,000 jobs” since the fourth quarter of 2016. That claim was false.

4 · The vote

Counties swung Republican

Each Ohio and Pennsylvania county is colored by the change in Republican presidential votes from 2012 to 2016. Coal-mining counties are outlined in brown. Hover over a county to see its 2012 and 2016 Republican vote totals.

Look at the Republican gains in the coal counties and the counties around them to see what a difference coal made.

A huge shift in Ohio and Pennsylvania

Republicans often aim to win enough rural voters, through both turnout and party switching, to offset Democratic strength in dense urban centers. In the swing states of Ohio and Pennsylvania, the coal-mining counties made a big difference to that strategy.

Pennsylvania had not voted for a Republican president since George H.W. Bush in 1988. Trump won it by 68,236 votes, netting 20 electoral votes. Turnout rose 6.3%, and the shift toward Republicans from 2012 was 6.1 points.

Ohio voted for Obama in both previous elections: 50.67% to Mitt Romney's 47.69% in 2012, and 51.38% to John McCain's 46.80% in 2008. The popular vote shifted 11.1 points from Democrat to Republican between 2012 and 2016, and Trump beat Clinton by 8.1%, earning 18 electoral votes.

Trump won the presidency with 304 electoral votes to Clinton's 227 after seven faithless electors broke ranks (the pledged result was 306 to 232); 270 are needed to win. Flipping just these two states would have given Clinton exactly 270 of the pledged votes, enough to win by the narrowest possible margin.

Sources & method

Maps are drawn with D3.js from U.S. Atlas TopoJSON using topojson-client. The state and county data was compiled by hand. Election figures come from Townhall.com and coal employment and production data from the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

The county election data covers Republican presidential votes in Ohio and Pennsylvania only. The Electoral College map shows pledged electors (Clinton 232, Trump 306). Maine splits its electors by congressional district: it is colored for Clinton, who won it statewide and took 3 of its 4 electors, while Trump took the other 1.
